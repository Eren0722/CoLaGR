                                                         Abstract
 1             Large language models (LLMs) are advancing sequential recommendation by cou-
 2             pling semantic knowledge with sequential user modeling. Recent studies seek to
 3             project sequential representations into the LLM semantic space, thereby incorpo-
 4             rating sequential information into semantic reasoning. However, these methods
 5             overlook a semantic-sequential heterogeneity gap (SSHG) between the sequential
 6             space and the semantic space, which weakens sequence retention during cross-
 7             space transfer and makes sequential utilization less stable. To address these issues,
 8             we propose EchoRec, a staged and effective large-small model collaboration frame-
 9             work for LLM-based sequential recommendation. EchoRec first uses a frozen LLM
10             to construct item and user semantic embeddings together with structured semantic
11             neighborhood tables. Guided by our information-theoretic sequence-retention anal-
12             ysis, EchoRec reshapes the sequential space through complementary contrastive
13             objectives, where item-level semantic perturbation learning reduces local seman-
14             tic metric mismatch, while user-level semantic-neighborhood learning mitigates
15             topological fragmentation. Subsequently, Sequence Injection (SI) is introduced as
16             a structure-aware transfer mechanism, injecting reshaped item embeddings into
17             the frozen LLM while aligning LLM-side user representations with teacher rep-
18             resentations. Extensive experiments on four datasets demonstrate that EchoRec
19             consistently outperforms all baselines, indicating that reshaped cross-space transfer
20             makes sequential cues more accessible and reliable for the LLM.


21   1    Introduction
22   With rapid advances in text understanding, contextual modeling, and open-domain reasoning, large
23   language models (LLMs) are increasingly viewed as promising foundation models for recommen-
24   dation [32, 42]. Their strong semantic capacity and unified modeling interface have motivated a
25   growing body of work on using LLMs for recommendation and text-rich sequential modeling [3, 23].
26   In sequential settings, recent studies further use LLMs as embedding generators or sequence-aware
27   recommenders [11, 10]. User interaction histories are therefore no longer treated only as sparse col-
28   laborative observations; they are also cast as inputs to semantic reasoning models that can potentially
29   capture richer behavioral patterns [12]. These developments highlight an important question: how to
30   introduce reliable sequential inductive bias into LLM-based recommendation without sacrificing the
31   semantic strengths of LLMs [10, 12].
32   Early efforts mainly adapted the LLM itself to recommendation through prompting and lightweight
33   tuning [38]. Explicit reasoning scaffolds and large-recommender reasoning frameworks further
34   extended this textual adaptation paradigm [8, 5]. As purely textual adaptation showed limits, later
35   studies reintroduced collaborative knowledge by aligning LLMs with pretrained sequential rec-

37   sequence-aware variants further emphasize preserving dynamic preferences during injection [12, 10].
38   Another line keeps the LLM outside the online decision loop as a semantic encoder or relation
39   provider for smaller sequential models [21, 22]. This offline semantic-enhancement direction also
40   uses relation-guided, alignment-oriented, and contrastive semantic pre-training to connect sequential
41   behavior with LLM-derived semantics [17, 25, 2]. Across these lines, online alignment and injection
42   methods aim to connect behavior-derived sequential spaces with LLM semantic spaces [18, 33],
43   while sequence-aware distillation and semantic-enhancement methods pursue the same goal from
44   complementary directions [10, 2].
45   However, the sequential space and the LLM semantic space obey different neighborhood rules.
46   The former reflects ID co-occurrence, popularity bias, and sparse interaction noise, whereas the
47   latter is organized by textual semantics and world knowledge [32]. We refer to this gap as the
48   Semantic-Sequential Heterogeneity Gap (SSHG). SSHG mainly appears in two forms that matter for
49   injection: local semantic metric mismatch, where semantic neighbors in the frozen LLM space are
50   not preserved in the raw SRec space, and semantic-neighborhood topological fragmentation, where
51   semantically similar users or sequences fail to form coherent local regions. Recent work on token-
52   level collaborative alignment points to incompatible local rules across these spaces [6]. LLM-based
53   view augmentation and cross-modal contrastive learning report related mismatches from different
54   angles [14, 24]. As a result, a lightweight projector may weaken transferable sequential cues when
55   forced to denoise, reorganize local structure, and align the sequential space at once. Recent diagnosis
56   reports the same symptom: LLM-based recommenders may rely on shortcut context rather than stable
57   sequential dependence [10, 7]. Reasoning-oriented recommendation studies further explore explicit
58   reasoning scaffolds, but such scaffolds do not by themselves establish whether sequential preference
59   is truly captured [8, 10].
60   From a cross-space transfer viewpoint, effective injection requires a teacher space whose local geom-
61   etry is compatible with the target semantic host. Inspired by recent large-small collaboration studies
62   in recommendation [9, 13], we propose EchoRec, a staged large-small collaborative recommendation
63   framework organized around reshape first, inject later. EchoRec first uses a frozen LLM to construct
64   offline semantic assets from items and user histories, and then applies Semantic-Aligned Contrastive
65   Pre-training (SACP) to the sequential teacher under next-item supervision together with item-level and
66   user-level semantic constraints, reducing local semantic metric mismatch and semantic-neighborhood
67   fragmentation in the teacher space. Sequence Injection (SI) then projects the purified teacher item
68   embeddings into the frozen LLM and aligns LLM-side user states with teacher representations,
69   transferring refined sequential structure with lower cross-space distortion.
70   Our contributions are summarized as follows:

71       • We propose EchoRec, a staged large-small collaboration framework for LLM-based sequential
72         recommendation. It first reshapes the teacher space with SACP and then injects refined sequential
73         structure into a frozen LLM under teacher alignment.
74       • We formalize SSHG in LLM-enhanced sequential recommendation, characterize it through local
75         semantic metric mismatch and semantic-neighborhood topological fragmentation, and show how
76         it hinders cross-space transfer.
77       • We provide an information-theoretic retention analysis showing how EchoRec reduces controllable
78         transfer loss, improving sequence retention and supporting more stable sequential utilization in
79         the LLM.
80       • Experiments on four benchmark datasets show that EchoRec consistently outperforms strong
81         baselines, improves alignment and structure transfer, and remains more stable under sequence
82         perturbation. Code is available at https://github.com/EchoRec/EchoRec.

 90   2.1   LLM4Rec setting

 91   In this paper, LLM4Rec refers to recommendation methods that keep the LLM in the online decision
 92   loop and use it to produce the recommendation output. Under this setting, user history is typically fed
 93   into the LLM via textual prompts or soft prompts. For feature-injection-based methods, collaborative
 94   representations from a small recommender are first mapped into the LLM hidden space and then used
 95   as contextual conditions during inference. Diagnostic evidence from prior work [10] suggests that
 96   existing LLM4Rec models exhibit limited sensitivity to sequential perturbation, indicating that “input
 97   history” is not equivalent to “stable sequential utilization.”

 98   2.2   Semantic-Sequential Heterogeneity Gap (SSHG)

 99   Let SCF denote the collaborative representation space learned by a conventional sequential recom-
100   mender, and let SLLM denote the semantic space intrinsic to the LLM. The former is primarily
101   shaped by ID co-occurrence statistics, whereas the latter is organized by semantic logic and language
102   priors. In this paper, we use Semantic-Sequential Heterogeneity Gap (SSHG) to denote the system-
103   atic mismatch between these two spaces that matters for cross-space injection. Concretely, SSHG
104   contains two coupled aspects: local semantic metric mismatch, where the nearest-neighbor structure
105   in the collaborative space SCF disagrees with that in SLLM , and semantic-neighborhood topological
106   fragmentation, where semantically similar users or sequences fail to form coherent neighborhoods.

115   3.1   Overview

117   LLM first provides stable semantic references for items and user histories. Stage I then trains a
118   sequential teacher with next-item supervision plus semantic constraints, so that the teacher remains
119   discriminative while its local neighborhoods become more compatible with the LLM semantic space.
120   Stage II freezes this enhanced teacher and injects its item-level signals into the frozen LLM, while
121   aligning LLM-side user states with the teacher-side user representation. Let Gsr and G+      sr denote the

181   3.5    Retention View and Training Pipeline

182   The pipeline above can be read as a sequence-retention mechanism. Appendix A formalizes this view
183   with local soft-neighborhood distributions, where the retained sequence signal is measured by how
184   much the injected LLM-side neighborhood deviates from the reshaped teacher-side neighborhood.
185   The resulting bound separates three sources of loss: teacher-space SSHG, a semantic-host residual,
186   and injection-time drift.
187   This decomposition explains the training order. SACP attacks the first term before transfer by using
188   frozen semantic neighborhoods as local references, so the teacher no longer presents SI with a raw
189   ID-dominated geometry. SI then attacks the drift term by aligning the LLM-side user representation
190   to the reshaped teacher while learning the ranking objective. The residual term records host-specific
191   mismatch that is not directly optimized, which is why our diagnosis separately measures pre-injection
192   alignment and post-injection structure transfer. Operationally, EchoRec follows three steps: build
193   frozen semantic assets, reshape the teacher with LSACP , and inject the enhanced teacher with LSI .

194   4     Experiments
195   We evaluate on four shared-benchmark datasets and ask:
196       • RQ1: How does EchoRec compare with directly comparable baselines on the shared benchmark?
197       • RQ2: Which components contribute most to the overall improvement?
198       • RQ3: Does EchoRec remain stable under inference-time sequence perturbation?
199       • RQ4: Does reducing SSHG improve alignment and structure transfer in controlled diagnosis?
200       • RQ5: Is EchoRec effective across different sequential teacher backbones?

201   4.1    Experimental setup

202   Datasets. We follow the shared benchmark protocol of Kim et al. [10] on four datasets: Movies,
203   Scientific, Electronics, and CDs. For each user, we adopt the standard leave-one-out sequential split.
204   Full dataset statistics are deferred to Appendix B.1.
205   Evaluation Metrics. During evaluation, each test case is ranked against 1 positive item + 99 negative
206   items. We report NDCG@10, NDCG@20, HR@10, and HR@20, and use validation NDCG@10 as
207   the checkpoint criterion.
208   Compared Methods. Under the same retrieval-style protocol, we compare EchoRec with nine
209   directly comparable baselines: GRU4Rec [31], BERT4Rec [35], NextItNet [37], SASRec [36],
210   TALLRec [38], LLaRA [18], A-LLMRec [19], CoLLM [33], and LLM-SRec [10]. Detailed grouping
211   and comparison notes are summarized in Appendix B.2.
212   Implementation Details. We follow the released protocols for directly comparable LLM4Rec
213   baselines. EchoRec is model-agnostic and can be integrated with various sequential recommendation
214   models. To introduce our approach, we adopt the Transformer architecture as the sequential teacher in
215   the main setting, following prior studies on sequential recommendation [26, 2]. Section 4.6 validates
216   the model-agnostic nature of EchoRec. Appendix B gives details.

217   4.2      Overall performance on the shared benchmark (RQ1)

218   Table 1 reports the unified benchmark comparison: the first four columns are conventional sequential
219   recommenders, the next six are directly comparable LLM4Rec methods, and the rightmost column
220   gives EchoRec’s gain over the strongest prior LLM4Rec baseline. EchoRec achieves the best results
221   on all datasets, with relative gains of 4.5%–16.4%. Two patterns emerge. Conventional SRec
222   models remain competitive, confirming the value of sequential inductive bias, but they do not use the
223   LLM host’s semantic reasoning. Prior LLM4Rec methods improve over prompt-only adaptation yet
224   still transfer raw collaborative geometry that can conflict with the LLM semantic space. EchoRec
225   combines both sides: SACP reshapes the teacher space with frozen semantic neighborhoods, and SI
226   injects a cleaner sequential signal. This is why the gains hold not only on NDCG, which rewards
227   top-rank ordering, but also on HR, which reflects candidate recall. The gains are especially visible on
228   CDs and Movies, where useful descriptions and sparse interactions make direct ID-space transfer
229   fragile. Sections 4.5 and 4.6 further show that the gains are not merely from stronger injection and
230   are not tied to one teacher backbone.

231   4.3      Ablation study (RQ2)

232   Table 2 shows a stable hierarchy across datasets. Removing teacher-side reshaping (w/o SACP)
233   causes the largest NDCG@10 and HR@10 drops: NDCG@10 falls from 0.4088 to 0.3697 on
234   Movies, 0.3822 to 0.3170 on Scientific, 0.3228 to 0.2756 on Electronics, and 0.4432 to 0.3733 on
235   CDs. This confirms that the main gain is not simply from adding an LLM-side projector; when the
236   teacher space remains noisy or fragmented, SI must both repair local structure and transfer across
237   spaces. The single-component removals are smaller but consistent. Item-CL reduces sensitivity
238   to exact ID substitutions, so semantically close items induce steadier sequence states. User-CL
239   smooths semantically related sparse or fragmented histories, improving neighborhood coherence
240   before transfer. Match keeps the injected LLM user state near the reshaped sequential subspace
241   instead of drifting toward generic semantic cues. Overall, the pattern supports a complementary
242   division of labor: SACP purifies the teacher space before transfer, and Match stabilizes the transferred
243   representation after injection.

244   4.4   Sequence perturbation stability (RQ3)

245   Figure 2 evaluates inference-time sequence perturbations on CDs under original, shuffle, reverse,
246   and recent-item removal settings. EchoRec keeps higher clean-input performance and degrades less
247   under moderate perturbations such as shuffle and recent-item removal. This suggests that EchoRec
248   does not merely memorize item IDs or prompt surface forms; the SACP teacher learns smoother
249   semantic neighborhoods, making small history changes less likely to cause abrupt drift after SI. Full
250   reversal still hurts both methods, as desired, because chronological preference evolution is central
251   to sequential recommendation. Thus, EchoRec better balances robustness and order sensitivity: it
252   tolerates moderate noise while still responding to severe temporal disruption. Additional Movies,
253   Scientific, and Electronics results are deferred to Appendix Figure 5.

254   4.5   Controlled SSHG diagnosis (RQ4)

255   With the dataset, LLM backbone, protocol, and budget fixed, Figure 3 isolates teacher-space reshaping
256   on CDs by comparing EchoRec with Pure-SI. EchoRec raises pre-injection semantic-neighborhood
257   overlap in panel (a) and yields higher post-injection structure transfer in panel (b). SACP reduces the
258   later projector’s burden: rather than mapping a raw ID-dominated teacher space into the LLM host,
259   SI receives a teacher space with local neighborhoods already more compatible with frozen semantic
260   neighborhoods. Thus, SI preserves teacher-side overlap and rank fidelity in the LLM space.
261   Table 3 extends this test to all datasets at k = 20: PreAlign@20 measures teacher–semantic
262   compatibility before SI, Jaccard@20 and RBO@20 measure preserved overlap and rank fidelity after

264   the “reshape first, inject later” pattern generalizes: alignment improves before injection, structure
265   preservation improves after injection, and ranking gains follow downstream. This supports our SSHG
266   interpretation that reducing teacher–semantic mismatch makes sequential cues more accessible to the
267   LLM host, rather than simply increasing capacity.

272   The gains hold for recurrent, self-attentive, and bidirectional encoders, indicating that teacher-space

274   5     Related Work

275   5.1   Sequential recommendation

276   Sequential recommendation mainly focuses on identifying similar items or users from collabora-
277   tive filtering signals and modeling how user preference evolves over interaction histories. Early
278   methods combined matrix factorization with Markov transitions to capture temporal dynamics, such
279   as FPMC [29]. Neural models then pushed this line forward: GRU4Rec [31] used recurrent ar-
280   chitectures, NextItNet [37] introduced convolutional sequence modeling, and later SASRec [36]
281   and BERT4Rec [35] further improved sequence representation with attention-based architectures.
282   Recent studies such as ICLRec [28], C2lRec [34], CMCLRec [24], CLLMRec [14], HPSERec [1],
283   and FMLP-Rec [27] continue to strengthen sequence learning through intent contrast, causal con-
284   trast, multimodal enhancement, LLM-based view augmentation, long-tail modeling, and lightweight
285   encoders. Despite these advances, sequential recommender spaces are still mainly shaped by ID
286   co-occurrence and exposure statistics, and therefore often lack sufficiently smooth and compatible
287   semantic structure when transferred into an LLM host.


288   5.2   LLM-based recommendation

289   With the rise of large language models, recommendation research has explored tighter ways of
290   coupling LLMs with sequential signals [32, 42]. Early efforts mainly adapted LLMs through
291   prompting or lightweight tuning: TALLRec [38] uses PEFT to bridge language modeling and
292   recommendation, while ThinkRec [8] further introduces explicit reasoning. As purely textual
293   adaptation showed clear limits, later work brought collaborative knowledge back into the LLM
294   pipeline. LLaRA [18] combines CF-SRec item embeddings with textual prompts, CoLLM [33]
295   directly injects collaborative embeddings into the online LLM context, and A-LLMRec [19] further
296   strengthens CF knowledge absorption through representation alignment. Recent work further exposes
297   weak dynamic-preference modeling in LLM4Rec and introduces sequence-aware distillation [10].
298   Another line uses LLMs to enhance smaller recommenders, including RLMRec [21], LRD [22],
299   LLM-ESR [25], SRA-CL [2], and LLMEmb [15], while related efforts explore collaborative retrieval
300   augmentation, plug-in LLM contrastive representations, logical or role-aware recommendation, and
301   token-level collaborative alignment [20, 16, 4, 6].
302   Inspired by recent large-small collaboration studies [9, 13], we argue that these two directions should
303   not be treated separately. Nevertheless, existing methods mainly focus on injection-time alignment or
304   distillation, largely overlooking structural mismatch between the teacher space and the LLM host.


305   6     Conclusion

306   This paper studies the Semantic-Sequential Heterogeneity Gap (SSHG) in LLM-enhanced sequential
307   recommendation and proposes EchoRec, which reshapes the SRec space with SACP before injecting
308   structure-aware signals via SI. We instantiate SSHG as local semantic metric mismatch and semantic-
309   neighborhood topological fragmentation, both targeted before injection. Experiments show consistent
310   gains over strong baselines, and controlled diagnosis confirms better SRec-semantic alignment and
311   local-structure preservation. Overall, reducing teacher-space mismatch and injection-time drift
312   improves sequence retention in the LLM space.

962   A.8   Relation to the Main-Text Diagnosis

963   The theoretical quantities above include KL-defined SSHG, semantic-host residual, and TV-type
964   retention error. In experiments, these continuous distributional quantities are not directly observable
965   from ranked retrieval outputs. We therefore use PreAlign@20, Jaccard@20, and RBO@20 as
966   empirical proxies for local structure preservation. These diagnostics should be read as evidence
967   consistent with the information-theoretic mechanism rather than as direct numerical verification of the
968   theorem. Because the LLM backbone, candidate pool, and SI architecture are fixed in the controlled
969   diagnosis, the observed structure-transfer gains remain consistent with the hypothesis that lowering
970   the controllable SSHG and drift terms improves retention, though the residual term is unmeasured.

971   A.9   Discussion: Perturbation Stability

972   This perturbation discussion should be interpreted as a local stability diagnosis rather than a global
973   robustness guarantee. The main text shows that EchoRec is more stable under moderate perturbations
974   such as shuffle and dropping recent interactions. From the same distributional viewpoint used above,
975   this pattern provides a possible explanation for the observed moderate-perturbation stability: if the
976   KL-defined SSHG is smaller before injection and the injection-time drift is better controlled, then the
977   transferred sequential structure may be more coherent under the clean input and less fragile under
978   moderate perturbations. This discussion is intended only to connect the retention view with the
979   empirical perturbation results; it is not used as a formal robustness theorem.
980   At the same time, the fact that EchoRec still changes under reverse should not be read as a contradic-
981   tion. On the contrary, it suggests that the model still preserves meaningful order sensitivity, rather
982   than degenerating into a spuriously order-invariant behavior. Full reversal is a strong perturbation
983   and should not be treated as part of this local regime. We therefore view the perturbation result as
984   qualitative evidence that cleaner teacher-side structure and better-controlled transfer are associated
985   with more stable, yet still sequence-sensitive, LLM representations.

1014   B.3   Implementation Details

1015   All reported experiments are completed on a workstation equipped with dual NVIDIA GeForce
1016   RTX 5090 GPUs. We use a LLaMA-3B checkpoint as the frozen LLM host and follow the shared
1017   benchmark protocol for data splitting, candidate construction, and evaluation. In Table 1, the SASRec
1018   column denotes the vanilla SASRec baseline, whereas EchoRec uses a Transformer-style sequential
1019   teacher in its two-stage pipeline. Stage I constructs frozen semantic assets offline with batch size 64
1020   and keeps Top-K semantic neighbors with K = 10 by default. The SACP teacher is trained with
1021   batch size 256, test batch size 512, Adam optimizer, and learning rate 10−3 . The default weights
1022   of the user-level and item-level contrastive terms are λu = 0.1 and λi = 0.1, respectively. Stage
1023   II freezes both the enhanced teacher and the LLM backbone, and optimizes only the lightweight
1024   projector, teacher-side mapper, and retrieval heads. Its default training batch size is 20 for Movies,
1025   Scientific, and CDs, and 16 for Electronics; the inference batch size is 10. We use Adam with
1026   learning rate 10−4 for SI, and set the teacher-matching weight to 1.0 by default. Hyperparameters
1027   and checkpoints are selected on the validation set using NDCG@10, while ablations only change the
1028   component under study. Section 4.6 further validates the model-agnostic nature of EchoRec.

1029   Prompt Templates. For readability, we keep the prompt templates in a compact form and separate
1030   the asset-construction prompts from the sequence-injection prompts.

1031   B.4   Efficiency and Computational Cost Discussion

1032   EchoRec follows a staged efficiency design. In Stage I, the frozen LLM is used only before teacher
1033   training to construct item/user semantic embeddings and semantic-neighborhood indices. These assets
1034   are cached and reused, so the LLM is not repeatedly queried or updated during SACP optimization.
1035   The additional Stage-I cost mainly comes from semantic-neighborhood lookup, semantic perturbation
1036   sampling, and the two contrastive losses, all of which are applied to the small sequential teacher
1037   rather than to the LLM.
1038   In Stage II, the LLM backbone remains frozen; only lightweight modules such as the projector,
1039   retrieval heads, and teacher-side mapper are optimized. Thus, the main Stage-II cost is the frozen
1040   LLM encoding of user/item prompts plus small alignment and ranking heads. This design avoids
1041   updating the LLM parameters while still allowing the reshaped teacher representations to enter the
1042   LLM retrieval space.

1061   D     Limitations
1062   Considering computational budgets and resource constraints, we evaluate EchoRec under a frozen-
1063   LLM retrieval setting with representative teacher backbones and shared benchmarks. While the
1064   same stage-wise design can be applied more broadly, results may vary with different LLM hosts,
1065   richer multimodal inputs, or alternative serving pipelines. EchoRec also uses offline semantic-asset
1066   construction and two-stage training, adding overhead over single-stage injection methods.

1067   E     Future Work
1068   Future work can study more efficient semantic-asset construction, parameter-efficient adaptation,
1069   and evaluation with a wider range of LLM hosts and teacher backbones. It is also worth extending
1070   the current setting to richer multimodal or online recommendation scenarios, so that large-small
1071   collaboration remains effective and practical in real-world pipelines.

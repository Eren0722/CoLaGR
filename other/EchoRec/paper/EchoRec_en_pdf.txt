                                                         Abstract
 1             Large language models (LLMs) are advancing sequential recommendation by cou-
 2             pling semantic knowledge with sequential user modeling. Recent studies seek to
 3             project sequential representations into the LLM semantic space, thereby incorpo-
 4             rating sequential information into semantic reasoning. However, these methods
 5             overlook a semantic-sequential heterogeneity gap (SSHG) between the sequential
 6             space and the semantic space, which weakens sequence retention during transfer
 7             across spaces and makes sequential utilization less stable. To address these issues,
 8             we propose EchoRec, a staged and effective large-small model collaboration frame-
 9             work for LLM-based sequential recommendation. EchoRec first uses a frozen LLM
10             to construct item and user semantic embeddings together with structured semantic
11             neighborhood tables. Guided by our information-theoretic sequence-retention anal-
12             ysis, EchoRec reshapes the sequential space through complementary contrastive
13             objectives: item-level semantic perturbation learning reduces local semantic metric
14             mismatch, while user-level semantic-neighborhood learning mitigates topological
15             fragmentation. Subsequently, Sequence Injection (SI) is introduced as a structure-
16             aware transfer mechanism, injecting reshaped item embeddings into the frozen
17             LLM while aligning LLM-side user representations with teacher representations.
18             Extensive experiments on four datasets demonstrate that EchoRec consistently
19             outperforms all baselines, indicating that reshaped cross-space transfer makes
20             sequential cues more accessible and reliable for the LLM.


21   1    Introduction
22   With rapid advances in text understanding, contextual modeling, and open-domain reasoning, large
23   language models (LLMs) are increasingly viewed as promising foundation models for recommen-
24   dation [32, 42]. Their strong semantic capacity and unified modeling interface have motivated a
25   growing body of work on using LLMs for recommendation and text-rich sequential modeling [3, 23].
26   In sequential settings, recent studies further use LLMs as embedding generators or sequence-aware
27   recommenders [11, 10]. User interaction histories are therefore no longer treated only as sparse col-
28   laborative observations; they are also cast as inputs to semantic reasoning models that can potentially
29   capture richer behavioral patterns [12]. These developments highlight an important question: how to
30   introduce reliable sequential inductive bias into LLM-based recommendation without sacrificing the
31   semantic strengths of LLMs [10, 12].
32   Early efforts mainly adapted the LLM itself to recommendation through prompting and lightweight
33   tuning [38]. Explicit reasoning scaffolds and large-recommender reasoning frameworks further
34   extended this textual adaptation paradigm [8, 5]. As purely textual adaptation showed limits, later
35   studies reintroduced collaborative knowledge by aligning LLMs with pretrained sequential rec-

36   ommenders [18, 19] or injecting collaborative signals into the LLM context [33, 39]. Recent
37   sequence-aware variants further emphasize preserving dynamic preferences during injection [12, 10].
38   Another line keeps the LLM outside the online decision loop as a semantic encoder or relation
39   provider for smaller sequential models [21, 22]. This offline semantic-enhancement direction also
40   uses relation-guided, alignment-oriented, and contrastive semantic pre-training to connect sequential
41   behavior with LLM-derived semantics [17, 25, 2]. Across these lines, online alignment and injection
42   methods aim to connect behavior-derived sequential spaces with LLM semantic spaces [18, 33],
43   while sequence-aware distillation and semantic-enhancement methods pursue the same goal from
44   complementary directions [10, 2].
45   However, the sequential space and the LLM semantic space obey different neighborhood rules.
46   The former reflects ID co-occurrence, popularity bias, and sparse interaction noise, whereas the
47   latter is organized by textual semantics and world knowledge [32]. We refer to this gap as the
48   Semantic-Sequential Heterogeneity Gap (SSHG). SSHG mainly appears in two forms that matter for
49   injection: local semantic metric mismatch, where semantic neighbors in the frozen LLM space are
50   not preserved in the raw SRec space, and semantic-neighborhood topological fragmentation, where
51   semantically similar users or sequences fail to form coherent local regions. Recent work on token-
52   level collaborative alignment points to incompatible local rules across these spaces [6]. LLM-based
53   view augmentation and cross-modal contrastive learning report related mismatches from different
54   angles [14, 24]. As a result, a lightweight projector may weaken transferable sequential cues when
55   forced to denoise, reorganize local structure, and align the sequential space at once. Recent diagnosis
56   reports the same symptom: LLM-based recommenders may rely on shortcut context rather than stable
57   sequential dependence [10, 7]. Reasoning-oriented recommendation studies further explore explicit
58   reasoning scaffolds, but such scaffolds do not by themselves establish whether sequential preference
59   is truly captured [8, 10].
60   From a cross-space transfer viewpoint, effective injection requires a teacher space whose local geom-
61   etry is compatible with the target semantic host. Inspired by recent large-small collaboration studies
62   in recommendation [9, 13], we propose EchoRec, a staged large-small collaborative recommendation
63   framework organized around reshape first, inject later. EchoRec first uses a frozen LLM to construct
64   offline semantic assets from items and user histories, and then applies Semantic-Aligned Contrastive
65   Pre-training (SACP) to the sequential teacher under next-item supervision together with item-level and
66   user-level semantic constraints, reducing local semantic metric mismatch and semantic-neighborhood
67   fragmentation in the teacher space. Sequence Injection (SI) then projects the purified teacher item
68   embeddings into the frozen LLM and aligns LLM-side user states with teacher representations,
69   transferring refined sequential structure with lower cross-space distortion.
70   Our contributions are summarized as follows:

71       • We propose EchoRec, a staged large-small collaboration framework for LLM-based sequential
72         recommendation. It first reshapes the teacher space with SACP and then injects refined sequential
73         structure into a frozen LLM under teacher alignment.
74       • We formalize SSHG in LLM-enhanced sequential recommendation, characterize it through local
75         semantic metric mismatch and semantic-neighborhood topological fragmentation, and show how
76         it hinders cross-space transfer.
77       • We provide an information-theoretic retention analysis showing how EchoRec reduces controllable
78         transfer loss, improving sequence retention and supporting more stable sequential utilization in
79         the LLM.
80       • Experiments on four benchmark datasets show that EchoRec consistently outperforms strong
81         baselines, improves alignment and structure transfer, and remains more stable under sequence
82         perturbation. Code is available at https://github.com/EchoRec/EchoRec.

 90   2.1   LLM4Rec setting

 91   In this paper, LLM4Rec refers to recommendation methods that keep the LLM in the online decision
 92   loop and use it to produce the recommendation output. Under this setting, user history is typically fed
 93   into the LLM via textual prompts or soft prompts. For feature-injection-based methods, collaborative
 94   representations from a small recommender are first mapped into the LLM hidden space and then used
 95   as contextual conditions during inference. Diagnostic evidence from prior work [10] suggests that
 96   existing LLM4Rec models exhibit limited sensitivity to sequential perturbation, indicating that “input
 97   history” is not equivalent to “stable sequential utilization.”

 98   2.2   Semantic-Sequential Heterogeneity Gap (SSHG)

 99   Let SCF denote the collaborative representation space learned by a conventional sequential recom-
100   mender, and let SLLM denote the semantic space intrinsic to the LLM. The former is primarily
101   shaped by ID co-occurrence statistics, whereas the latter is organized by semantic logic and language
102   priors. In this paper, we use Semantic-Sequential Heterogeneity Gap (SSHG) to denote the system-
103   atic mismatch between these two spaces that matters for cross-space injection. Concretely, SSHG
104   contains two coupled aspects: local semantic metric mismatch, where the nearest-neighbor structure
105   in the collaborative space SCF disagrees with that in SLLM , and semantic-neighborhood topological
106   fragmentation, where semantically similar users or sequences fail to form coherent neighborhoods.

114   3     Method
115   3.1   Overview

116   Given user set U, item set I, and a time-ordered interaction sequence Su = [iu1 , iu2 , . . . , iuTu ] for user
117   u, EchoRec predicts yu through a two-stage large-small collaboration pipeline in which a frozen
118   LLM provides semantic assets to reshape the sequential teacher, and the enhanced teacher transfers
119   refined sequential structure back into the LLM. The method consists of semantic asset preparation,
120   teacher-side Semantic-Aligned Contrastive Pre-training (SACP), and Sequence Injection (SI). Let

217   3.5   A Distributional View of Cross-Space Transfer

218   To make the stage-wise design explicit, we analyze EchoRec through local neighborhood distributions
219   rather than raw hidden vectors. For each user u, let NuK denote a shared candidate set, and let psem
                                                                                                       u ,
220   pseq
       u   , and pllm
                  u   denote  the local neighborhood distributions induced by the frozen semantic,  SACP-
221   enhanced sequential, and injected LLM spaces. We focus on preserving teacher-side sequential
222   structure after injection. Appendix A defines SSHG as the relative entropy between the teacher-side


                                                          6
223   sequential neighborhood distribution and the frozen semantic reference, and shows that sequence-
224   retention error is controlled by three terms: KL-defined SSHG, semantic-host residual, and injection-
225   time representation drift. In this view, SACP targets the controllable SSHG term through a local
226   teacher–semantic score-distortion surrogate, while SI with Lmatch reduces drift by keeping the
227   injected LLM representation close to the teacher-aligned subspace. Accordingly, Stage I improves
228   teacher-space transferability, Stage II improves injection fidelity, and reducing the controllable terms
229   lowers an upper bound on sequence-retention error in the LLM host.

230   3.6    Training pipeline and rationale

231   EchoRec follows a simple two-stage pipeline. We first freeze Ellm to build semantic assets and
232   Top-K semantic neighborhoods from item texts and textualized user histories, then train Gsr with
233   LSACP to obtain the enhanced teacher G+  sr and its item embeddings, and finally freeze this teacher
234   while optimizing the lightweight SI modules on prompts that contain [HistoryEmb], [UserOut], and
235   [ItemOut]. The rationale behind the staged design is that direct injection from the raw collaborative
236   space would force the projector to denoise, repair local structure, and align modalities simultaneously;
237   EchoRec instead reduces this burden through SACP before SI completes the final transfer.

238   4     Experiments
239   Research Questions

240   We evaluate on four shared-benchmark datasets and ask:

241       • RQ1: How does EchoRec compare with directly comparable baselines on the shared benchmark?
242       • RQ2: Which components contribute most to the overall improvement?
243       • RQ3: Does EchoRec remain stable under inference-time sequence perturbation?
244       • RQ4: Does reducing SSHG improve alignment and structure transfer in controlled diagnosis?

245   4.1    Experimental setup

246   Datasets. We follow the shared benchmark protocol of Kim et al. [10] on four datasets: Movies,
247   Scientific, Electronics, and CDs. For each user, we adopt the standard leave-one-out sequential split.
248   Full dataset statistics are deferred to Appendix B.1.
249   Evaluation Metrics. During evaluation, each test case is ranked against 1 positive item + 99 negative
250   items. We report NDCG@10, NDCG@20, HR@10, and HR@20, and use validation NDCG@10 as
251   the checkpoint criterion.
252   Compared Methods. Under the same retrieval-style protocol, we compare EchoRec with nine
253   directly comparable baselines: GRU4Rec [31], BERT4Rec [35], NextItNet [37], SASRec [36],
254   TALLRec [38], LLaRA [18], A-LLMRec [19], CoLLM [33], and LLM-SRec [10]. Detailed grouping
255   and comparison notes are summarized in Appendix B.2.
256   Implementation Details. We follow the released protocols for directly comparable LLM4Rec
257   baselines. EchoRec is model-agnostic and can be integrated with various sequential recommendation
258   models. To introduce our approach, we adopt the Transformer architecture as the sequential teacher
259   in the main setting, following prior studies on sequential recommendation [26, 2]. Appendix C.1
260   validates the model-agnostic nature of EchoRec. Appendix B gives details.

298   5      Related Work
299   5.1      Sequential recommendation

300   Sequential recommendation mainly focuses on identifying similar items or users from collabora-
301   tive filtering signals and modeling how user preference evolves over interaction histories. Early
302   methods combined matrix factorization with Markov transitions to capture temporal dynamics, such
303   as FPMC [29]. Neural models then pushed this line forward: GRU4Rec [31] used recurrent ar-
304   chitectures, NextItNet [37] introduced convolutional sequence modeling, and later SASRec [36]
305   and BERT4Rec [35] further improved sequence representation with attention-based architectures.
306   Recent studies such as ICLRec [28], C2lRec [34], CMCLRec [24], CLLMRec [14], HPSERec [1],
307   and FMLP-Rec [27] continue to strengthen sequence learning through intent contrast, causal con-
308   trast, multimodal enhancement, LLM-based view augmentation, long-tail modeling, and lightweight
309   encoders. Despite these advances, sequential recommender spaces are still mainly shaped by ID
310   co-occurrence and exposure statistics, and therefore often lack sufficiently smooth and compatible
311   semantic structure when transferred into an LLM host.

312   5.2      LLM-based recommendation

313   With the rise of large language models, recommendation research has explored tighter ways of
314   coupling LLMs with sequential signals [32, 42]. Early efforts mainly adapted LLMs through
315   prompting or lightweight tuning: TALLRec [38] uses PEFT to bridge language modeling and
316   recommendation, while ThinkRec [8] further introduces explicit reasoning. As purely textual
317   adaptation showed clear limits, later work brought collaborative knowledge back into the LLM
318   pipeline. LLaRA [18] combines CF-SRec item embeddings with textual prompts, CoLLM [33]
319   directly injects collaborative embeddings into the online LLM context, and A-LLMRec [19] further
320   strengthens CF knowledge absorption through representation alignment. Recent work further exposes
321   weak dynamic-preference modeling in LLM4Rec and introduces sequence-aware distillation [10].
322   Another line uses LLMs to enhance smaller recommenders, including RLMRec [21], LRD [22],


                                                                              9
323   LLM-ESR [25], SRA-CL [2], and LLMEmb [15], while related efforts explore collaborative retrieval
324   augmentation, plug-in LLM contrastive representations, logical or role-aware recommendation, and
325   token-level collaborative alignment [20, 16, 4, 6]. Inspired by recent large-small collaboration
326   studies [9, 13], we argue that these two directions should not be treated separately. Nevertheless,
327   existing methods mainly focus on injection-time alignment or distillation, largely overlooking
328   structural mismatch between the teacher space and the LLM semantic host.

329   6   Conclusion
330   This paper studies the Semantic-Sequential Heterogeneity Gap (SSHG) in LLM-enhanced sequential
331   recommendation and proposes EchoRec, a staged framework that first reshapes the SRec space and
332   then injects structure-aware signals. We instantiate SSHG through local semantic metric mismatch
333   and semantic-neighborhood topological fragmentation, and design SACP to mitigate them before
334   SI. Experiments show consistent gains over strong baselines, while controlled diagnosis further
335   indicates better SRec-semantic alignment and more faithful preservation of local SRec structure after
336   injection into the LLM space. Overall, the results support reducing sequence-retention error through
337   teacher-space pre-alignment together with injection-time drift control.

 987   A.8   Relation to the Main-Text Diagnosis

 988   The theoretical quantities above include KL-defined SSHG, semantic-host residual, and TV-type
 989   retention error. In experiments, these continuous distributional quantities are not directly observable
 990   from ranked retrieval outputs. We therefore use PreAlign@20, Jaccard@20, and RBO@20 as
 991   empirical proxies for local structure preservation. These diagnostics should be read as evidence
 992   consistent with the information-theoretic mechanism rather than as direct numerical verification of the
 993   theorem. Because the LLM backbone, candidate pool, and SI architecture are fixed in the controlled
 994   diagnosis, the observed structure-transfer gains remain consistent with the hypothesis that lowering
 995   the controllable SSHG and drift terms improves retention, though the residual term is unmeasured. zz

 996   A.9   Discussion: Perturbation Stability

 997   This perturbation discussion should be interpreted as a local stability diagnosis rather than a global
 998   robustness guarantee. The main text shows that EchoRec is more stable under moderate perturbations
 999   such as shuffle and dropping recent interactions. From the same distributional viewpoint used above,
1000   this pattern provides a possible explanation for the observed moderate-perturbation stability: if the
1001   KL-defined SSHG is smaller before injection and the injection-time drift is better controlled, then the
1002   transferred sequential structure may be more coherent under the clean input and less fragile under
1003   moderate perturbations. This discussion is intended only to connect the retention view with the
1004   empirical perturbation results; it is not used as a formal robustness theorem.
1005   At the same time, the fact that EchoRec still changes under reverse should not be read as a contradic-
1006   tion. On the contrary, it suggests that the model still preserves meaningful order sensitivity, rather
1007   than degenerating into a spuriously order-invariant behavior. Full reversal is a strong perturbation
1008   and should not be treated as part of this local regime. We therefore view the perturbation result as
1009   qualitative evidence that cleaner teacher-side structure and better-controlled transfer are associated
1010   with more stable, yet still sequence-sensitive, LLM representations.

1039   B.3   Implementation Details

1040   All reported experiments are completed on a workstation equipped with dual NVIDIA GeForce
1041   RTX 5090 GPUs. We use a LLaMA-3B checkpoint as the frozen LLM host and follow the shared
1042   benchmark protocol for data splitting, candidate construction, and evaluation. In Table 1, the SASRec
1043   column denotes the vanilla SASRec baseline, whereas EchoRec uses a Transformer-style sequential
1044   teacher in its two-stage pipeline. Stage I constructs frozen semantic assets offline with batch size 64
1045   and keeps Top-K semantic neighbors with K = 10 by default. The SACP teacher is trained with
1046   batch size 256, test batch size 512, Adam optimizer, and learning rate 10−3 . The default weights
1047   of the user-level and item-level contrastive terms are λu = 0.1 and λi = 0.1, respectively. Stage
1048   II freezes both the enhanced teacher and the LLM backbone, and optimizes only the lightweight
1049   projector, teacher-side mapper, and retrieval heads. Its default training batch size is 20 for Movies,
1050   Scientific, and CDs, and 16 for Electronics; the inference batch size is 10. We use Adam with
1051   learning rate 10−4 for SI, and set the teacher-matching weight to 1.0 by default. Hyperparameters
1052   and checkpoints are selected on the validation set using NDCG@10, while ablations only change the
1053   component under study. Appendix C.1 further validates the model-agnostic nature of EchoRec.

1054   Prompt Templates. For readability, we keep the prompt templates in a compact form and separate
1055   the asset-construction prompts from the sequence-injection prompts.

1056   B.4   Efficiency and Computational Cost Discussion

1057   EchoRec follows a staged efficiency design. In Stage I, the frozen LLM is used only before teacher
1058   training to construct item/user semantic embeddings and semantic-neighborhood indices. These assets
1059   are cached and reused, so the LLM is not repeatedly queried or updated during SACP optimization.
1060   The additional Stage-I cost mainly comes from semantic-neighborhood lookup, semantic perturbation
1061   sampling, and the two contrastive losses, all of which are applied to the small sequential teacher
1062   rather than to the LLM.
1063   In Stage II, the LLM backbone remains frozen; only lightweight modules such as the projector,
1064   retrieval heads, and teacher-side mapper are optimized. Thus, the main Stage-II cost is the frozen
1065   LLM encoding of user/item prompts plus small alignment and ranking heads. This design avoids
1066   updating the LLM parameters while still allowing the reshaped teacher representations to enter the
1067   LLM retrieval space.

1074   C.1     Validation of Model-Agnostic Characteristic

1075   In this section, we validate the model-agnostic nature of our method. The main text adopts the
1076   Transformer architecture as the sequential teacher to facilitate the introduction of the proposed
1077   framework; here we further test EchoRec on different teacher backbones. We therefore select
1078   three classic sequential recommendation models (GRU4Rec, SASRec, and BERT4Rec) as teacher
1079   backbones and integrate EchoRec under the same SI protocol. We retain the original backbone
1080   architectures and compare the full EchoRec pipeline against Pure-SI with the same LLM host and
1081   evaluation setting. The current results on CDs and Movies are shown in Figures 4 and 5. For both
1082   datasets, the EchoRec-enhanced versions consistently outperform the corresponding Pure-SI variants
1083   across all three backbone methods.
1084   On CDs, BERT4Rec improves by 20.5% on NDCG@10 and 12.4% on HR@10; SASRec improves
1085   by 14.0% and 10.0%; and GRU4Rec improves by 10.2% and 9.5%. On Movies, the same pattern
1086   persists: BERT4Rec improves by 9.3% and 7.6%, SASRec by 8.4% and 7.4%, and GRU4Rec by
1087   10.3% and 6.9%. Together, EchoRec does not rely on one particular sequential encoder, but robustly
1088   improves transferred sequential signals across multiple teacher families and datasets.

1101   D      Limitations
1102   Considering computational budgets and resource constraints, we evaluate EchoRec under a frozen-
1103   LLM retrieval setting with representative teacher backbones and shared benchmarks. While the
1104   same stage-wise design can be applied more broadly, results may vary with different LLM hosts,
1105   richer multimodal inputs, or alternative serving pipelines. EchoRec also uses offline semantic-asset
1106   construction and two-stage training, adding overhead over single-stage injection methods.

1107   E    Future Work
1108   Future work can study more efficient semantic-asset construction, parameter-efficient adaptation,
1109   and evaluation with a wider range of LLM hosts and teacher backbones. It is also worth extending
1110   the current setting to richer multimodal or online recommendation scenarios, so that large-small
1111   collaboration remains effective and practical in real-world pipelines.

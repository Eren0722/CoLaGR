                                                         Abstract
 1             Large language models (LLMs) are advancing sequential recommendation by
 2             coupling semantic knowledge with sequential user modeling. Recent studies
 3             seek to project sequential representations into the LLM semantic space, thereby
 4             incorporating sequential information into semantic reasoning. However, these
 5             methods overlook a semantic-sequential heterogeneity gap (SSHG) between the
 6             sequential space and the semantic space, which weakens sequence retention during
 7             cross-space transfer and makes sequential utilization less robust. To address these
 8             issues, we propose EchoRec, a staged large-small model collaboration framework
 9             for LLM-based sequential recommendation. EchoRec first uses a frozen LLM to
10             construct item and user semantic embeddings together with structured semantic-
11             neighborhood tables. Based on information theory, we analyze sequence retention
12             and construct complementary item-level and user-level contrastive objectives to
13             reshape the sequential space. Subsequently, Sequence Injection (SI) is introduced
14             as a structure-aware transfer mechanism, injecting reshaped item embeddings
15             into the frozen LLM while aligning LLM-side user representations with teacher
16             representations. Extensive experiments on four benchmark datasets demonstrate
17             that EchoRec consistently outperforms all baselines, indicating that reshaped cross-
18             space transfer makes sequential cues more effective and robust for the LLM.


19   1    Introduction
20   With rapid advances in text understanding, contextual modeling, and open-domain reasoning, large
21   language models (LLMs) are increasingly viewed as promising foundation models for recommen-
22   dation [33, 42]. Their strong semantic capacity and unified modeling interface have motivated a
23   growing body of work on using LLMs for recommendation and text-rich sequential modeling [2, 25].
24   In sequential settings, recent studies further use LLMs as embedding generators or sequence-aware
25   recommenders [13, 17]. User interaction histories are therefore no longer treated only as sparse col-
26   laborative observations; they are also cast as inputs to semantic reasoning models that can potentially
27   capture richer behavioral patterns [14]. These developments highlight an important question: how to
28   introduce reliable sequential inductive bias into LLM-based recommendation without sacrificing the
29   semantic strengths of LLMs [17, 14].
30   Early efforts mainly adapted the LLM itself to recommendation through prompting and lightweight
31   tuning [38]. Explicit reasoning scaffolds and large-recommender reasoning frameworks further
32   extended this textual adaptation paradigm [11, 7]. As purely textual adaptation showed limits,
33   later studies reintroduced collaborative knowledge by aligning LLMs with pretrained sequential
34   recommenders [20, 21] or injecting collaborative signals into the LLM context [34, 39]. Recent
35   sequence-aware variants further emphasize preserving dynamic preferences during injection [14, 17].

36   Another line keeps the LLM outside the online decision loop as a semantic encoder or relation
37   provider for smaller sequential models [23, 24]. This offline semantic-enhancement direction also
38   uses relation-guided, alignment-oriented, and contrastive semantic pre-training to connect sequential
39   behavior with LLM-derived semantics [19, 26, 15]. Across these lines, online alignment and injection
40   methods aim to connect behavior-derived sequential spaces with LLM semantic spaces [20, 34],
41   while sequence-aware distillation and semantic-enhancement methods pursue the same goal from
42   complementary directions [17, 15].
43   However, the sequential space and the LLM semantic space obey different neighborhood rules.
44   The former reflects ID co-occurrence, popularity bias, and sparse interaction noise, whereas the
45   latter is organized by textual semantics and world knowledge [33]. We refer to this gap as the
46   Semantic-Sequential Heterogeneity Gap (SSHG). SSHG mainly appears in two forms that matter for
47   injection: local semantic metric mismatch, where semantic neighbors in the frozen LLM space are
48   not preserved in the raw SRec space, and semantic-neighborhood topological fragmentation, where
49   semantically similar users or sequences fail to form coherent local regions. Recent work on token-
50   level collaborative alignment points to incompatible local rules across these spaces [3]. LLM-based
51   view augmentation and cross-modal contrastive learning report related mismatches from different
52   angles [8, 6]. As a result, a lightweight projector may weaken transferable sequential cues when
53   forced to denoise, reorganize local structure, and align the sequential space at once. Recent diagnosis
54   reports the same symptom: LLM-based recommenders may rely on shortcut context rather than stable
55   sequential dependence [17, 9]. Reasoning-oriented recommendation studies further explore explicit
56   reasoning scaffolds, but such scaffolds do not by themselves establish whether sequential preference
57   is truly captured [11, 17].
58   To address the above problems, we propose EchoRec, a staged large-small collaborative recom-
59   mendation framework for robust LLM-based sequential recommendation. Our method follows a
60   reshape-first, inject-later design to make the teacher space compatible with the target semantic host
61   before cross-space transfer. EchoRec first uses a frozen LLM to construct offline semantic assets
62   from items and user histories. It then introduces Semantic-Aligned Contrastive Pre-training (SACP),
63   which trains the sequential teacher with next-item supervision and complementary item-level and
64   user-level semantic constraints, reducing local semantic metric mismatch and semantic-neighborhood
65   fragmentation in the teacher space. Finally, Sequence Injection (SI) projects the reshaped teacher
66   item embeddings into the frozen LLM and aligns LLM-side user states with teacher representations,
67   transferring refined sequential structure with lower cross-space distortion.
68   Our contributions are summarized as follows:
69       • We propose EchoRec, a staged large-small collaboration framework for LLM-based sequential
70         recommendation. It first reshapes the teacher space with SACP and then injects refined sequential
71         structure into a frozen LLM under teacher alignment.
72       • We formalize SSHG in LLM-enhanced sequential recommendation, characterize it through local
73         semantic metric mismatch and semantic-neighborhood topological fragmentation, and address it
74         with item-level perturbation and user-level semantic-neighborhood learning.
75       • We provide an information-theoretic retention analysis that decomposes transfer loss into SSHG,
76         semantic-host residual, and injection-time drift, showing how EchoRec improves sequence
77         retention and stabilizes sequential utilization in the LLM.
78       • Experiments on four benchmark datasets show that EchoRec consistently outperforms strong
79         baselines, improves alignment and structure transfer, and remains more stable under sequence
80         perturbation. Code is available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/EchoRec/.

 91   2.1   LLM4Rec setting

 92   In this paper, LLM4Rec refers to recommendation methods that keep the LLM in the online decision
 93   loop and use it to produce the recommendation output. Under this setting, user history is typically fed
 94   into the LLM via textual prompts or soft prompts. For feature-injection-based methods, collaborative
 95   representations from a small recommender are first mapped into the LLM hidden space and then used
 96   as contextual conditions during inference. Diagnostic evidence from prior work [17] suggests that
 97   existing LLM4Rec models exhibit limited sensitivity to sequential perturbation, indicating that “input
 98   history” is not equivalent to “stable sequential utilization.”

 99   2.2   Semantic-Sequential Heterogeneity Gap (SSHG)

100   Let SCF denote the collaborative representation space learned by a conventional sequential recom-
101   mender, and let SLLM denote the semantic space intrinsic to the LLM. The former is primarily shaped
102   by ID co-occurrence statistics, whereas the latter is organized by semantic logic and language priors.
103   From an information-theoretic perspective, we analyze this mismatch as the Semantic-Sequential
104   Heterogeneity Gap (SSHG), a structural source of sequence-retention loss during cross-space in-
105   jection. Concretely, SSHG contains two coupled aspects: local semantic metric mismatch, where
106   the nearest-neighbor structure in the collaborative space SCF disagrees with that in SLLM , and
107   semantic-neighborhood topological fragmentation, where semantically similar users or sequences fail
108   to form coherent neighborhoods.

116   3     Method
117   3.1   Overview

118   The analysis above shows that direct cross-space injection is unreliable because the raw sequential
119   teacher must preserve next-item discrimination while also facing semantic metric mismatch and
120   topological fragmentation. To address these problems, we propose EchoRec, a staged large-small
121   collaboration framework organized around reshape first, inject later. In the preparation stage, a frozen
122   LLM provides stable semantic references for items and user histories. In Stage I, EchoRec trains a
123   sequential teacher with next-item supervision and semantic constraints, preserving recommendation
124   ability while reshaping local neighborhoods toward the LLM semantic space. In Stage II, EchoRec
125   freezes the enhanced teacher, injects its refined item-level signals into the frozen LLM, and aligns
126   LLM-side user states with teacher-side user representations. Thus, online training is restricted to
127   lightweight transfer modules, while the LLM remains a fixed semantic host.

190   3.5    Retention View and Training Pipeline

191   The pipeline above can be read as a sequence-retention mechanism. Appendix A formalizes this view
192   with local soft-neighborhood distributions, where the retained sequence signal is measured by how
193   much the injected LLM-side neighborhood deviates from the reshaped teacher-side neighborhood.
194   The resulting bound separates retention loss into three sources: teacher-space SSHG, a semantic-host
195   residual, and injection-time drift.
196   This decomposition explains the training order. SACP attacks the first term before transfer by using
197   frozen semantic-neighborhoods as local references, so the teacher no longer presents SI with a raw
198   ID-dominated geometry. SI then attacks the drift term by aligning the LLM-side user representation
199   to the reshaped teacher while learning the ranking objective. The residual term records host-specific
200   mismatch that is not directly optimized, which is why our diagnosis separately measures pre-injection
201   alignment and post-injection structure transfer. Operationally, EchoRec follows three steps: build
202   frozen semantic assets, reshape the teacher with LSACP , and inject the enhanced teacher with LSI .

203   4     Experiments
204   We evaluate on four shared-benchmark datasets and ask:
205       • RQ1: How does EchoRec compare with directly comparable baselines on the shared benchmark?
206       • RQ2: Which components contribute most to the overall improvement?
207       • RQ3: Is EchoRec robust under inference-time sequence perturbation?
208       • RQ4: Does reducing SSHG improve alignment and structure transfer in controlled diagnosis?
209       • RQ5: Is EchoRec effective across different sequential teacher backbones?

210   4.1    Experimental setup

211   Datasets. We follow the shared benchmark protocol of Kim et al. [17] on four datasets: Movies,
212   Scientific, Electronics, and CDs. For each user, we adopt the standard leave-one-out sequential split.
213   Full dataset statistics are deferred to Appendix B.1.
214   Evaluation Metrics. Each test case is ranked against 1 positive item + 99 negatives. We report
215   NDCG@10/20 and HR@10/20 for top-K ranking, and additionally report PreAlign@20, Jaccard@20,
216   and RBO@20 for SSHG diagnosis; definitions are in Appendix B.2.
217   Compared Methods. Under the same retrieval-style protocol, we compare EchoRec with nine
218   directly comparable baselines: GRU4Rec [32], BERT4Rec [35], NextItNet [37], SASRec [36],
219   TALLRec [38], LLaRA [20], A-LLMRec [21], CoLLM [34], and LLM-SRec [17]. Detailed grouping
220   and comparison notes are summarized in Appendix B.3.
221   Implementation Details. We follow the released protocols for directly comparable LLM4Rec
222   baselines. EchoRec is model-agnostic and can be integrated with various sequential recommendation
223   models. To introduce our approach, we adopt the Transformer architecture as the sequential teacher in
224   the main setting, following prior studies on sequential recommendation [27, 15]. Section 4.6 provides
225   model-agnostic evaluation. Appendix B.4 gives details.

226   4.2      Overall performance on the shared benchmark (RQ1)

227   Table 1 reports the unified benchmark comparison: the first four columns are conventional sequential
228   recommenders, the next six are directly comparable LLM4Rec methods, and the rightmost column
229   gives EchoRec’s gain over the strongest prior LLM4Rec baseline. EchoRec achieves the best results
230   on all datasets, with relative gains of 4.5%–16.4%. Conventional SRec models remain competitive
231   but lack the LLM host’s semantic reasoning, while prior LLM4Rec methods still transfer raw
232   collaborative geometry that can conflict with the LLM semantic space. EchoRec combines both
233   sides: SACP reshapes the teacher space with frozen semantic-neighborhoods, and SI injects a cleaner
234   sequential signal. The gains hold on both NDCG and HR, indicating improvements in both top-rank
235   ordering and candidate recall. The margin over LLM-SRec is meaningful because LLM-SRec already
236   performs sequence-aware injection, suggesting that reshape-first reduces transfer difficulty before
237   LLM-side alignment. The gains are especially visible on CDs and Movies, where sparse interactions
238   and useful item text make direct ID-space transfer fragile. Sections 4.5 and 4.6 further show that the
239   gains are not merely from stronger injection and are not tied to one teacher backbone.

240   4.3      Ablation study (RQ2)

241   Table 2 shows a stable hierarchy across datasets. Removing teacher-side reshaping (w/o SACP)
242   causes the largest drops: NDCG@10 falls from 0.4088 to 0.3697 on Movies, 0.3822 to 0.3170 on
243   Scientific, 0.3228 to 0.2756 on Electronics, and 0.4432 to 0.3733 on CDs. This confirms that the
244   main gain is not simply from adding an LLM-side projector; when the teacher space remains noisy or
245   fragmented, SI must both repair local structure and transfer across spaces. The single-component
246   removals are smaller, indicating complementary roles. Item-CL reduces sensitivity to exact ID
247   substitutions, User-CL improves neighborhood coherence for related histories, and Match keeps the
248   injected LLM user state near the reshaped sequential subspace. Overall, SACP purifies the teacher
249   space before transfer, while Item-CL, User-CL, and Match refine SI transfer without replacing the
250   reshape-first stage that prepares the teacher for reliable injection.

251   4.4   Sequence perturbation stability (RQ3)

252   Figure 2 evaluates inference-time sequence perturbations on CDs under original, shuffle, reverse,
253   and recent-item removal settings. EchoRec keeps higher clean-input performance and degrades less
254   under moderate perturbations such as shuffle and recent-item removal. Randomized perturbations
255   are repeated with seeds 42, 52, and 62 and show the same trend. This suggests that EchoRec
256   does not merely memorize item IDs or prompt surface forms; the SACP teacher learns smoother
257   semantic-neighborhoods, making small history changes less likely to cause abrupt drift after SI. Full
258   reversal still hurts both methods, as desired, because chronological preference evolution is central
259   to sequential recommendation. Thus, EchoRec better balances robustness and order sensitivity: it
260   tolerates moderate noise while still responding to severe temporal disruption. Additional Movies,
261   Scientific, and Electronics results are deferred to Appendix C.1.

281   5      Related Work

282   Sequential Recommendation. Sequential recommendation mainly focuses on identifying similar
283   items or users from collaborative filtering signals and modeling how user preference evolves over
284   interaction histories. Early methods combined matrix factorization with Markov transitions to capture
285   temporal dynamics, such as FPMC [30]. Neural models then pushed this line forward: GRU4Rec [32]
286   used recurrent architectures, NextItNet [37] introduced convolutional sequence modeling, and later
287   SASRec [36] and BERT4Rec [35] further improved sequence representation with attention-based
288   architectures. Recent studies such as ICLRec [29], C2lRec [10], CMCLRec [6], CLLMRec [8],
289   HPSERec [1], and FMLP-Rec [28] continue to strengthen sequence learning through intent contrast,
290   causal contrast, multimodal enhancement, LLM-based view augmentation, long-tail modeling, and
291   lightweight encoders. These methods improve sequential discrimination but usually optimize within
292   the collaborative space itself. Despite these advances, sequential recommender spaces are still mainly
293   shaped by ID co-occurrence and exposure statistics, often lacking smooth and compatible semantic
294   structure for transfer into an LLM host.
295   LLM-based Recommendation. With the rise of large language models, recommendation research
296   has explored tighter ways of coupling LLMs with sequential signals [33, 42]. Early efforts mainly
297   adapted LLMs through prompting or lightweight tuning: TALLRec [38] uses PEFT to bridge language
298   modeling and recommendation, while ThinkRec [11] further introduces explicit reasoning. As purely
299   textual adaptation showed clear limits, later work brought collaborative knowledge back into the
300   LLM pipeline. LLaRA [20] combines CF-SRec item embeddings with textual prompts, CoLLM [34]
301   directly injects collaborative embeddings into the online LLM context, and A-LLMRec [21] further
302   strengthens CF knowledge absorption through representation alignment. Recent work further exposes
303   weak dynamic-preference modeling in LLM4Rec and introduces sequence-aware distillation [17].
304   Another line uses LLMs to enhance smaller recommenders, including RLMRec [23], LRD [24],
305   LLM-ESR [26], SRA-CL [15], LLMEmb [16], and large-small collaboration methods [12, 5], while
306   related efforts explore collaborative retrieval augmentation [22], plug-in LLM contrastive representa-
307   tions [18], logical or role-aware recommendation [4], and token-level collaborative alignment [3].
308   Inspired by recent large-small collaboration studies, we argue that these two directions should not
309   be treated separately. Nevertheless, existing methods mainly focus on injection-time alignment
310   or distillation, largely overlooking structural mismatch between the teacher space and the LLM
311   host. EchoRec differs by using frozen LLM semantics as a reference to reshape the small teacher
312   before transfer, so collaboration is organized as a staged compatibility problem rather than a one-shot
313   alignment problem.


314   6      Conclusion

315   In this work, we study SSHG in LLM-enhanced sequential recommendation and propose EchoRec, a
316   staged large-small collaboration framework. EchoRec builds frozen semantic assets, reshapes the
317   sequential teacher with SACP, and injects the refined teacher space into the frozen LLM through SI.
318   Experiments, ablations, perturbation analysis, SSHG diagnosis, and model-agnostic studies show
319   consistent gains and confirm that preparing a compatible teacher space improves sequence transfer
320   before injection. These findings suggest that sequence retention should be treated as a structural
321   compatibility problem rather than only an injection-time alignment problem. Overall, EchoRec
322   supports a reshape-first, inject-later strategy for robust LLM-based sequential recommendation.

636   A.9   Discussion: Sequence-Perturbation Robustness

637   The perturbation results characterize EchoRec’s robustness under moderate input-history perturba-
638   tions. The main text shows that EchoRec is more stable under shuffle and dropping recent interactions.
639   From the same distributional viewpoint used above, this pattern connects perturbation robustness
640   with the retention mechanism: a smaller KL-defined SSHG before injection and better-controlled
641   injection-time drift support a more coherent transferred sequential structure under the clean input and
642   lower fragility under moderate perturbations.
643   EchoRec’s response to reverse perturbation further shows that the model preserves meaningful order
644   sensitivity instead of collapsing into spuriously order-invariant behavior. Since full reversal disrupts
645   chronological preference evolution, the perturbation results demonstrate that EchoRec improves
646   robustness to moderate noise while retaining sequence sensitivity under severe temporal disruption.

714   B.5   Efficiency and Computational Cost Discussion

715   EchoRec follows a staged efficiency design. In Stage I, the frozen LLM is used only before teacher
716   training to construct item/user semantic embeddings and semantic-neighborhood indices. These assets
717   are cached and reused, so the LLM is not repeatedly queried or updated during SACP optimization.
718   The additional Stage-I cost mainly comes from semantic-neighborhood lookup, semantic perturbation
719   sampling, and the two contrastive losses, all of which are applied to the small sequential teacher
720   rather than to the LLM.
721   In Stage II, the LLM backbone remains frozen; only lightweight modules such as the projector,
722   retrieval heads, and teacher-side mapper are optimized. Thus, the main Stage-II cost is the frozen
723   LLM encoding of user/item prompts plus small alignment and ranking heads. This design avoids
724   updating the LLM parameters while still allowing the reshaped teacher representations to enter the
725   LLM retrieval space.
726   At inference time, EchoRec does not run SACP, construct semantic-neighborhoods, or evaluate
727   contrastive objectives. The deployed path only keeps the enhanced teacher item table, projection
728   module, frozen LLM, and retrieval heads. Thus, most additional computation is shifted to offline or
729   training-time stages, enabling better structure modeling and sequence retention while keeping the
730   online path lightweight.

745   EchoRec is evaluated under a frozen-LLM retrieval setting with representative teacher backbones and
746   shared benchmarks. The stage-wise design is compatible with different LLM hosts, richer multimodal
747   inputs, and alternative serving pipelines. EchoRec uses offline semantic-asset construction and
748   two-stage training, shifting additional computation to reusable preprocessing and training stages
749   while keeping online inference lightweight.

750   E     Broader Impacts and Assets
751   EchoRec may improve LLM-based sequential recommendation by making transferred sequential cues
752   more reliable. Potential negative impacts include amplifying historical popularity bias, exposing user-
753   preference patterns if interaction logs are mishandled, and increasing computation compared with
754   lightweight recommenders. Our experiments use public benchmark datasets and cited baseline/model
755   assets; the released code will document reproduction details and asset terms.
